Verifiable Docking: Repairing Only What Needs to Move

A docking pose can be almost right and still clash with the protein. Can the clash itself tell us what to repair?

Kirato Yoshihara

Introduction

A docking model can put a molecule in roughly the right place and still push one of its atoms into the protein. The pose is close to the experimental structure, but it fails a physical check. The usual fix is force-field relaxation. It works well enough that it changed the question behind this project.

Can a failed check tell us which parts of the molecule need to move? The goal is to keep the useful parts of a pose while giving the problematic regions just enough freedom to move.

On a fresh PoseX holdout, multi-component repair preserved ordinary relaxation’s observed top-5 joint success with less displacement. Minimization was 3.14× faster by the ratio of medians; recorded repair wall time improved by 1.08×. Choosing what moves helped, but most of the speedup disappeared outside the minimizer.

We’ll start with a quick picture of docking, then follow the experiments from local repair and matched controls to learned scope selection—and see how much of the speedup survives across the whole pipeline.

Docking, in one picture

A ligand is a molecule that binds to a larger molecule; here, it is a small molecule binding to a protein. Docking asks for its bound pose: position, orientation, and internal conformation. Those choices are coupled. Rotating a bond can clear one clash and create another.

Different methods get to that pose differently. The distinction matters here because our intervention happens after a pose has been predicted.

Three routes to a predicted pose. These are methodological examples, not a performance ranking.

Generate posesDiffDock [5]Use diffusion over translation, rotation, and torsion.

Our repair experiments start from DiffDock predictions. We evaluate accuracy against a reference and physical plausibility with the full PoseBusters suite [6], whose checks use the RDKit cheminformatics toolkit [7].

Top-5 joint success means at least one of the top five poses is both near-native and valid. Passing physical checks does not prove that the ligand binds in that pose.

A strong baseline

We began with the public PoseX benchmark [8]. Of 433 initially near-native but invalid poses, ordinary relaxation made 410 valid—about 95%. That is recovery within the repair cohort, rather than an overall docking success rate.

A fresh DiffDock run told the same story: ordinary relaxation raised top-5 joint success from 41 to 77 of 200 cases. So we shifted the question toward preserving that utility while moving less of the molecule.

Choosing what moves

A clash supplies more than a failed check: it identifies a concrete location. We call that violation a counterexample, or witness. The proposal is to use it to choose a repair scope, relax that scope, and check the result again.

Before choosing a rule, we asked an oracle how small a successful scope could be, using global OpenMM relaxation as the comparator [9]. An overly strict scope missed repairs. Expanding through coupled constraints helped; a factor graph represents those local dependencies [10].

Table 1. How much freedom does repair need? Exploratory results on 106 poses.
ScopeValid posesWhat it tells us

That is encouraging headroom, but this was an already observed, RMSD-selected cohort. The broader evaluation did not establish non-inferiority. An oracle shows what might be possible; we still need a rule that chooses the scope without seeing the answer.

Conceptual repair cascade · Python pseudocode
# Schematic helpers, not a library API.
def repair(pose, protein):
    witnesses = find_clashes(pose, protein)
    scope = choose_components(pose, witnesses)
    local = relax(pose, protein, movable=scope)
    assert unchanged_outside(pose, local, scope)

    if verifier_passes(local, protein):
        candidate = local
    else:
        candidate = ordinary_relaxation(pose, protein)
    return candidate

The deterministic verifier routes the cascade; full PoseBusters validity is evaluated on the output. These checks have separate roles. Reference RMSD does not enter this selection rule.

The control that moved

Would any similar scope work? On 166 poses, guided and random repair both made 160 valid under soft restraints. The experiment failed its locked specificity criterion. A control scope could still repair the real clash because atoms outside the scope were free to move against a soft penalty.

Let x^0 be the starting coordinates and F the atoms outside the movable set, including the protein. The soft implementation adds a quadratic tether to the prepared system’s potential, E_0:

E_{\mathrm{soft}}(x)=E_0(x)+\frac{\kappa}{2}\sum_{i\in F}\lVert x_i-x_i^0\rVert_2^2

A finite penalty still lets an atom move if the rest of the energy decreases enough. Hard freezing instead constrains the optimization:

\begin{aligned}\min_x\;&E_0(x)\\\text{subject to}\;&x_i=x_i^0\quad(i\in F)\end{aligned}

Selected heavy atoms bring their attached hydrogens into the movable set. The hard boundary uses zero-mass frozen particles in OpenMM. All arms in this diagnostic used a 100-iteration cap; the constrained minimum describes the objective, not a guarantee of global convergence.

Under soft restraints, even an excluded clash atom moved by roughly 0.36 Å in the random control. Under hard freezing, the maximum frozen-heavy-atom displacement was zero. Once the boundary held, the true witness mattered.

This supports a role for location information under the tested intervention. It does not reverse the failed soft experiment: hard freezing was a post-hoc mechanism diagnostic, and its top-5 joint success still fell below ordinary relaxation.

Covering several clashes

A single anchor can be informative and still miss a problem elsewhere in the ligand. On its locked holdout, about half the poses needed fallback, making the full procedure slower. The next operator covered several implicated regions at once.

The multi-component operator groups connected clash atoms, chooses an anchor for each group, and unions their local neighborhoods. After finding a passing pose x^1, it can shorten the edit by moving back toward the input:

\begin{gathered}x(\alpha)=x^0+\alpha(x^1-x^0),\\0\leq\alpha\leq1.\end{gathered}

A short bisection search keeps a verifier-passing step. This reduces unnecessary motion along that path; it does not find a globally minimal edit.

The new PoseX holdout had no pair or ligand-group overlap with earlier splits. Its repair cohort contained 127 poses. This tests fresh ligands and predictions; it does not establish transfer to unseen protein families.

The full cost of repair

The cascade’s minimization time improved by 3.14×, but recorded repair wall time improved by only 1.08×. The first compares median minimization times; the second divides summed wall times. Both include ordinary fallback where needed.

This is the kind of limit behind Amdahl’s argument [11]: other costs remain when one part becomes faster. Detection and verification still take time. The two ratios cannot give us a timing breakdown, but they make the next profiling target clear: the repair as a whole.

Recorded wall time covers the repair call and initial clash detection. Docking, preparation, and surrounding scope construction are outside this comparison.

Learning the scope

The early MLP pilot did not establish a witness benefit. A separate development study, WISAN, found a more promising signal with a factor-graph GNN. It ranked a frozen, witness-independent library of candidate scopes, evaluated out of fold across 43 disjoint families.

Sparse-Valid@3: verify up to three ranked candidates, stopping at the first Exact-20 pass. Count success only if that accepted scope moves at most 30% of ligand heavy atoms.

Exact-20 is this study’s endpoint. It is distinct from the earlier full PoseBusters endpoint, and does not establish near-native docking accuracy.

Both witness contrasts had positive family-bootstrap lower bounds. That supports useful location-dependent selection. The practical targets tell us what is still missing.

The model learns validity and two cost scores: coordinate edit and work. The supplied implementation combines classification, pairwise ranking, cost regression, and calibration in one loss:

\begin{aligned}\mathcal L={}&\mathcal L_{\mathrm{wBCE}}+\mathcal L_{\mathrm{rank}}\\&+0.25\,\mathcal L_{\mathrm{cost}}+0.1\,\mathcal L_{\mathrm{Brier}}\end{aligned}

Weighted binary cross-entropy handles label imbalance; the Brier term measures probability error. The cost term fits log-scaled edit and work with Huber losses on valid candidates. Pairwise ranking uses a score that penalizes scope size and both predicted costs:

\begin{aligned}u_c={}&z_c-a_c\\&-\operatorname{ReLU}(r_{d,c})-\operatorname{ReLU}(r_{w,c}).\end{aligned}

Here z_c is the validity logit, a_c the active heavy-atom fraction, and r_{d,c},r_{w,c} the log-cost head outputs. Training pairs prefer valid candidates first, then smaller scopes, edits, and work:

\mathcal L_{\mathrm{rank}}=\underset{(c^+,c^-)\in\mathcal P}{\operatorname{mean}}\operatorname{softplus}(u_{c^-}-u_{c^+}).

At inference, candidates with seed-averaged validity probability \bar p_c\geq0.25 are sorted by this tuple, from left to right:

\operatorname{key}(c)=\left(a_c,\widehat d_c,\widehat w_c,-\bar p_c,\operatorname{id}_c\right).

The edit and work scores are averaged, ReLU-clamped head outputs. The verifier makes the final acceptance decision. Sparsity and cost therefore already enter training and selection; they were simply not enough to meet the practical targets in this run. And better ranking cannot repair poses whose library contains no valid candidate.

What still needs to travel

Generalization needs a different split, not just another random sample. DockGen tests novel binding domains [12]; PoseBench evaluates broader docking settings [13]. Our external studies also used Astex [14] and CASP15 ligand tasks [15].

External confirmation is still missing. The broader policy experiments ran into lower utility, false accepts, insufficient cohorts, or failed specificity criteria. Those results keep the claim narrower than “local repair works everywhere.”

Table 4. The next experiments.
QuestionNext step
Does repair transfer?Freeze the multi-component operator; test an external family-disjoint holdout.
Can the whole repair be faster?Profile detection, selection, verification, and fallback with minimization.
Can the learned selector be useful?Evaluate docking accuracy and reduce active fraction and work before confirmation.

A failed physical check can tell us something useful about the intervention to make. Hard-freeze controls show why location matters; multi-component repair gives that information a useful role. Making it faster overall, sparse when learned, and reliable on unfamiliar proteins is the work still ahead.
